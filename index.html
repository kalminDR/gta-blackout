Views of a game nobody can play

19 November Should Be an Ordinary Thursday

Grand Theft Auto VI arrives on a Thursday. Not a holiday. Not a weekend. A working day, with payroll running, schools open and the trains on their normal timetable.

And several million people have spent years waiting for it. We want to know whether that shows up anywhere else — on the roads, in the offices, in the games people abandon, in the money. Nobody has ever measured what an event like this does to an ordinary day. So we are counting, every hour, from now until it happens.

What we will look for

The interesting question is not how many people will play it. Of course millions will. The question is whether a single game can bend a working day out of shape — enough that it shows up in traffic, in offices, in what people stop doing, and eventually in money.

Every day has a shape. Roads fill in the morning, games fill in the evening, and the pattern repeats so reliably that you can predict it. Below is that shape, drawn from our own readings. On 19 November we find out whether it holds.

So we watch for changes in ordinary routines. Some are direct: Twitch viewing, other games on Steam, road congestion, transit ridership, service health, online activity during working hours. Some are reported by readers themselves: taking annual leave, calling in sick, or turning up and expecting to get less done.

Those are not the same kind of evidence. We can measure traffic and player counts. We can count self-reports. We cannot see every absence or know why a particular person stayed home. No public dataset records GTA-related sick leave.

If commuting is quieter, if other games lose players, or if services come under unusual load, those are signals. They are not proof of motive on their own. The point is to see how many independent signals move together, and to say plainly where observation ends and inference begins.

Method

Since 2 September 2026, an automated collector has taken hourly readings from public sources covering Twitch, Steam, road congestion, PlayStation Network and Xbox Live service health, official YouTube counters, Hacker News, console resale prices and prediction markets. Historical baselines come from New York transit ridership and Wikipedia editing across six languages, back to 2023.

New York's subway carries about 4.5 million people on an ordinary November Thursday; comparable Thursdays vary by under one percent, so a drop of roughly 90,000 riders would be clearly detectable.

We tested GitHub and Stack Overflow as productivity measures, rejected both, and publish the evidence. Every reading is timestamped in a public repository.

Get the results

Two emails, then silence. In October, we publish our forecast before release, while there is nothing to fit the story to. In late November, we send the results. Publishing first means we cannot rewrite it afterwards to flatter the outcome.

One game can make people plan ahead, coordinate with friends, rearrange schedules and give hours of concentrated attention without a manager, deadline or reminder. Organisations routinely struggle to get the same adults through thirty minutes of mandatory training.

That contrast is why [eureka], the learning-and-development consultancy behind Grand Theft Attention, built this project. We make serious games and gamified training because attention is not a side issue. We are interested in what earns it, keeps it and changes when people give it voluntarily.

The problem is not that a game earns people's attention. It is that most training does not.
